Yoga for Stress and Sleep: An Evening Wind-Down Routine

How yoga for stress and sleep works: slow movement, calming breathing techniques, a simple evening wind-down routine, and when to seek professional support.

By Carey Caratini, founder of Space Pilates + Wellness Society. Pilates instructor, NASM certified personal trainer and licensed mental health therapist.

Yoga can help you wind down by pairing slow, gentle movement with deliberate breathing, which many people find settles both body and mind. A short, consistent evening routine of supported poses and an extended exhale gives your day a clear ending point. It is a wellness practice, not a treatment, and it works best alongside good sleep habits and professional care when you need it.

Key takeaways

  • Slow movement and slow breathing give your body a clear signal that it is time to downshift.
  • A 10 to 15 minute evening routine, done most nights, can become a reliable part of your bedtime.
  • Extended-exhale breathing and box breathing are simple techniques you can use anywhere.
  • Gentle, supported poses suit the evening better than vigorous flows.
  • Yoga is not a substitute for mental health care or medical treatment. If stress, anxiety or sleep problems persist, reach out to a licensed professional.

Why slow movement and breath help you wind down

Most of us carry the day in our bodies: tight shoulders, a clenched jaw, shallow breathing. Slow movement gives those places a chance to soften.

Breathing is the other half. When you deliberately slow your breath and lengthen your exhale, many people notice they feel calmer. That is one reason breath is woven through every yoga class, and why it is the most useful tool you can carry into your evening.

There is also the simple value of a ritual. Doing the same short sequence at the same time each night creates a transition between the busy part of your day and rest. Over time, the routine itself becomes a cue.

What kind of yoga fits the evening

Not every style suits bedtime. A brisk vinyasa flow raises your heart rate and can leave you feeling energized, which is great in the morning and less helpful at 10 PM.

For the evening, choose:

  • Supported poses that let props or the floor carry your weight.
  • Slow transitions with no jumping or fast sequences.
  • Longer holds, where you stay for a minute or more and let your breath slow.
  • Gentle stretches for the hips, back and shoulders, where tension tends to collect.

Restorative yoga is built on these principles. You can read how it compares to more active styles in restorative yoga vs vinyasa.

A simple evening wind-down routine

This sequence takes about 10 to 15 minutes. Do it on a mat, a rug or even your bed. Use pillows and blankets as props. Move slowly and keep your breath smooth throughout. If anything causes pain, skip it.

  1. Seated breathing (2 minutes). Sit comfortably, cross-legged or on a pillow. Rest your hands on your thighs. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four and out for a count of six. Let your shoulders drop a little on each exhale.
  2. Neck and shoulder release (1 minute). Gently tilt your right ear toward your right shoulder and hold for a few breaths, then switch sides. Finish with a few slow shoulder rolls.
  3. Cat and cow (1 minute). Come to hands and knees. Inhale as you lift your chest and tailbone, exhale as you round your back. Move slowly, following your breath.
  4. Child's pose (2 minutes). Sit your hips back toward your heels with knees wide and rest your forehead on the floor or a pillow. Let your arms rest forward or alongside you.
  5. Reclined twist (1 minute per side). Lie on your back, draw your knees in, and let them fall to one side. Open your arms wide. Let gravity do the work, then switch sides.
  6. Legs up the wall (3 to 5 minutes). Sit sideways next to a wall, then swing your legs up as you lie back. Rest your arms by your sides. If this is uncomfortable, rest your calves on a chair or the edge of the bed instead.
  7. Final rest (2 minutes). Lie flat, cover yourself with a blanket, and let your breath return to its natural rhythm. Notice the weight of your body on the floor.

Keep the lights low and your phone in another room if you can. A shorter version done most nights beats a long one done occasionally.

Breathing techniques to try

You do not need a mat for these. Use them in bed, in the car before walking inside, or any time you want to downshift.

Extended exhale

Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of four. Breathe out slowly, through your nose or softly pursed lips, for a count of six to eight. Repeat for a few minutes.

Keep it comfortable. If the counts feel like a strain, shorten them. The aim is simply an exhale that is a little longer than the inhale.

Box breathing

Breathe in for a count of four. Hold gently for four. Breathe out for four. Hold for four. Repeat for several rounds.

The steady, even count gives your mind something simple to follow. If the holds feel uncomfortable, shorten them or skip them and just breathe in and out evenly. For more on how breath is used across disciplines, see breathwork in Pilates and yoga.

Habits that support the routine

Yoga works best as one part of a calmer evening, not as a fix on its own. A few habits that pair well with it:

  • Keep a similar bedtime and wake time, even on weekends where you can.
  • Dim lights and step away from screens for a while before bed.
  • Keep your bedroom cool, dark and quiet.
  • Notice how caffeine and late meals affect you, and adjust if needed.

A regular class also helps. An hour of guided, unhurried movement can be the pause between everything else.

When to seek professional support

Yoga is not a substitute for mental health care or medical care. It is a general wellness practice, not a remedy for anxiety, depression, insomnia or any other condition.

If stress, anxiety, low mood or sleep problems persist, interfere with your daily life, or feel hard to manage, reach out to a licensed mental health professional or your doctor. Asking for support is a strong and practical step. If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, available 24 hours a day in the US.

If you have a medical condition, an injury or are pregnant, check with your doctor before starting a new movement routine, and let your instructor know so they can offer modifications.

Questions

What is the best time to do yoga for sleep?

Many people find a gentle routine in the hour before bed works well. Keep it slow and supported, and save more vigorous practice for earlier in the day.

Will yoga fix my insomnia?

No. Yoga is not a medical or clinical approach to insomnia or any sleep disorder. It can be a helpful part of a relaxing evening routine, but ongoing sleep problems are worth discussing with your doctor.

How long should an evening yoga routine be?

Ten to fifteen minutes is plenty. A short routine you do most nights tends to be more useful than a long one you rarely get to.

Is box breathing or extended exhale better?

Both are simple and widely used. Try each for a week and notice which feels more natural to you. Many people prefer an extended exhale at bedtime because it involves no breath holds.
